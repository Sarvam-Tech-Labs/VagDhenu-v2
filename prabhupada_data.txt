Computational Pipelines for Zero-Shot Voice Cloning and Sanskrit Prosodic Modeling in Historical Audio ArchivesIntroduction to the Synthesis of Historical Sanskrit RecitationThe endeavor to construct a text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis model capable of ingesting arbitrary Sanskrit verses and rendering them in the exact acoustic profile and prosodic meter of a historical figure represents a highly complex intersection of natural language processing (NLP), historical audio restoration, and computational prosody. Specifically, the task of cloning the voice of His Divine Grace A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada to generate novel Sanskrit verse recitations requires overcoming significant acoustic and linguistic hurdles. The foundational requirement for any neural voice cloning architecture is high-quality, phonetically transcribed audio aligned precisely with its corresponding text. For this specific project, the dataset must exclusively isolate instances of verse recitation from conversational speech, lecture delivery, or musical accompaniment.Standard conversational TTS models predict phoneme duration based on semantic context and natural speech cadence. However, classical Sanskrit poetry is rigidly governed by prosody (chhandas), which dictates the exact temporal duration (weight) of every syllable. Failure to explicitly condition the TTS model on this meter will result in a voice that simply reads the verse like prose, entirely losing the rhythmic, devotional quality of the chanting. Furthermore, the source audio for this target speaker is captured on vintage magnetic tape cassettes spanning the years 1966 to 1977. These recordings are inherently characterized by high noise floors, tape flutter, and instrumental bleed, such as the harmonium or karatala (hand cymbals).This comprehensive report details the exhaustive methodology required to acquire, annotate, restore, and align this highly specific dataset. It evaluates the exact digital archives available online that provide the necessary audio-text pairs, delineates the programmatic scraping methodologies to harvest this data, outlines the audio separation and denoising pipelines, and culminates in the architectural design of a prosodically aware, neural TTS model capable of capturing the precise metrical chanting of the target historical speaker.Identification and Acquisition of Annotated Source MaterialThe primary bottleneck in historical voice cloning is the acquisition of ground-truth data, wherein the audio is perfectly annotated with its textual counterpart. The user requires data where the target speaker is explicitly chanting a particular verse, accompanied by annotations indicating the exact text being chanted. Fortunately, the exhaustive digitization efforts of the Bhaktivedanta Archives and related collaborative platforms offer a highly structured, extensively annotated repository of audio-text pairs that perfectly fulfill this requirement.The primary corpora for this project reside in two highly indexed digital ecosystems: Vedabase, maintained by the Bhaktivedanta Archives, and the Vanipedia and Vanisource platforms. The audio recordings preserve the sound of the speaker's voice across lectures, conversations, morning walks, bhajans, Sanskrit recitations, and dictations spanning a critical eleven-year period from 1966 to 1977. Transcripts on Vedabase alone total over 3,703 entries, structured chronologically and categorized by document type, including major texts like the Bhagavad-gita (452 transcript entries), Srimad-Bhagavatam (944 transcript entries), and Caitanya-caritamrta (125 transcript entries).The Vanisource Verse Recitation ArchivesWhile parsing entire hour-long lectures to find isolated Sanskrit chanting is computationally inefficient, the Vanipedia/Vanisource platform provides a pre-curated solution that directly addresses the requirements of this specific TTS project.The platform features a meticulously categorized metadata repository titled "His Reciting Bhagavad-gita Verses". This directory organizes audio snippets into 18 distinct sub-categories, corresponding to each chapter of the Bhagavad-gita (e.g., "Reciting BG Chapter 01 Verses", "Reciting BG Chapter 02 Verses", through Chapter 18). Crucially, these pages contain isolated audio clips explicitly paired with the textual verse being chanted. This eliminates the need for manual cropping of full lecture audio files, providing a ready-made dataset of paired audio and Sanskrit text.Beyond the Bhagavad-gita, the archives contain dictation recordings where the speaker formally dictated translations and Sanskrit vocabulary for the Krsna Book (including Chapters 64 through 65 and 72 through 90). These dictations provide high-fidelity, studio-like acoustic environments with minimal background audience noise. While the prosody of a dictation differs from rhythmic chanting, incorporating this data significantly improves the TTS model's base acoustic modeling and phonetic coverage.Additionally, the "Nectar Drops" collection offers a curated repository of over 2,100 short audio excerpts, each under 90 seconds in duration, drawn from recorded lectures, conversations, and morning walks. These excerpts frequently contain isolated philosophical aphorisms or short verse recitations, serving as a valuable supplementary dataset for expanding the acoustic variety of the training corpus.The Audio to Text Fidelity ProjectA critical failure point in TTS dataset creation is transcription inaccuracy. Discrepancies between the audio and the text, such as false starts, coughs, skipped words, or mid-sentence pauses, destroy the convergence of forced alignment algorithms. The dataset must possess an exceptionally high degree of textual fidelity to ensure the acoustic model correctly maps spectrogram frames to phonemes.The "Srila Prabhupada's Audio to Text Fidelity Project," an initiative launched by Vanipedia on April 19, 2014, serves as an invaluable resource for securing pristine ground-truth data. The primary goal of this project is the comprehensive proofreading and restoration of all original transcriptions corresponding to the 4,000 extant audio files, representing approximately 1,500 hours of listening time.The protocols followed by this volunteer-driven project directly benefit the computational pipeline for dataset construction. Proofreaders systematically handle inaudible sections, previously marked as (?) or (indistinct), and meticulously map ellipses (...) to represent pauses or mid-sentence thought switches. Furthermore, they standardize spelling to American conventions and correct spacing errors around punctuation.Most crucially for this TTS project, any scriptural quotes or Sanskrit verses spoken in the audio are explicitly enriched in the text by attaching standard reference tags within brackets. For example, a chanted verse is annotated with tags such as (BG 1.1) for the Bhagavad-gita, (SB 1.1.1) for the Srimad-Bhagavatam, or (CC Adi 1.1) for the Caitanya-caritamrta. For a machine learning engineer, these tags function as ideal regular expression (Regex) anchors. A computational pipeline can utilize these tags to automatically locate the Sanskrit text in the digital library, verify its presence in the transcript, and slice the corresponding audio segment based on timestamps, thereby automating the generation of the verse-specific dataset.Programmatic Scraping and Data Pipeline MethodologiesTo construct the dataset at scale, developers must deploy programmatic web scraping methodologies to interface with these digital archives. The open-source community provides significant foundations for this effort through various GitHub repositories that have already mapped the Vedabase and Vanipedia structures.Repositories such as dwivedi-ai/srimadbhagvat_rag demonstrate functional web scrapers written in Python that fetch Srimad Bhagavatam purports and verses directly from vedabase.io. This specific repository utilizes a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) architecture, scraping the texts, converting them into vector embeddings using sentence-transformers, and storing them in a persistent local vector database like ChromaDB. A similar architecture is found in the swayyaam/anugamana-backend repository, which builds an AI-powered semantic search system for the Bhagavad-gita As It Is.Furthermore, the raw texts themselves are available in highly structured formats. The iskconpress/books and vaniwiki/prabhupada-books GitHub repositories contain the original texts formatted in DokuWiki syntax, which closely resembles Creole, a universal wiki syntax specification. These repositories represent the texts in a clean, programmatic format, entirely devoid of HTML clutter, making them ideal for ingestion into a natural language processing pipeline.By combining HTML parsing libraries like Beautiful Soup with the structural logic of these existing repositories, a researcher can construct a pipeline that systematically crawls lecture transcripts filtered by the "Bhagavad-gita" or "Srimad-Bhagavatam" document types. The algorithm can identify the occurrence of scriptural tags (e.g., (BG 2.13)), fetch the corresponding Devanagari or IAST (International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration) text block, download the associated .mp3 file from the source domain, and output a highly structured JSON or CSV file pairing the verse text with the lecture audio URL and contextual metadata.Data SourceContent TypologyRelevance to TTS PipelineStructural MetadataVanisource "Reciting BG Verses"Isolated Audio + Verse TextPrimary training data for rhythmic chantingGrouped sequentially by Chapter 1-18Vedabase TranscriptsFull Lectures and PurportsContextual phonetic variety and backgroundFilterable by Date, Location, and Text typePrabhupada-Books GitHubRaw DokuWiki Text filesClean text corpus for language modeling (LM)Pre-cleaned markdown / syntax specificationAudio to Text FidelityHuman-Verified TranscriptsPristine ground-truth for forced alignmentEmploys explicit scriptural tags (e.g., BG 1.1)Audio Pre-Processing and Vintage RestorationHistorical recordings captured during the 1960s and 1970s present severe acoustic challenges for modern neural acoustic models. If a TTS engine is trained on noisy data, it will inevitably hallucinate that noise during inference, permanently embedding magnetic tape hiss, room reverberation, and instrumental background into the synthesized voice. Therefore, before the extracted verse audio can be aligned with the text, it must undergo aggressive, state-of-the-art audio restoration.Neural Source SeparationIn many instances within the archives, Sanskrit verses are chanted in a call-and-response format (kirtan) or accompanied by traditional instruments, most notably the harmonium and the mridangam (a resonant two-headed drum). To isolate the target speaker's voice for cloning, neural network-based audio separation architectures must be deployed to eliminate harmonic interference.Tools such as Ultimate Vocal Remover (UVR5) and Facebook's Demucs represent the industry standard for musical source separation. Demucs utilizes a complex U-Net architecture operating over both waveform and spectrogram domains to split audio into multi-stem components, typically categorizing signals into vocals, drums, bass, and other. Because the harmonium possesses a rich harmonic structure that frequently overlaps with the fundamental frequencies of human speech, traditional EQ filtering is entirely ineffective. By processing the historical archive recordings through the Demucs algorithm, the system can extract the isolated vocal stem, completely suppressing the harmonium and ambient audience noise, ensuring that the acoustic model learns only the phonetic characteristics of the speaker's vocal tract.Dynamic Denoising and Spectral RestorationSubsequent to instrumental stem separation, the isolated vocal track inevitably retains the inherent noise floor of the vintage recording medium. This includes continuous magnetic tape hiss, electrical hum (mains hum from 60Hz or 50Hz power supplies), and low-frequency rumbling. Conventional noise gates or simple spectral subtraction methods degrade speech quality, causing audible artifacts known as "musical noise" or phase distortion, which are highly detrimental to TTS training.AI-driven solutions designed specifically for voice enhancement must be applied. Architectures like DeepFilterNet or similar local, open-source spectral denoisers utilize deep learning to model the statistical distribution of noise versus speech, effectively separating the two even in low signal-to-noise ratio environments. These models analyze the temporal and spectral dependencies of speech, removing flutter and electrical hum without compromising the integrity of the vocal formants.Speaker DiarizationThe pedagogical style of the target speaker frequently involves reciting a line of a Sanskrit verse, immediately followed by the audience or disciples repeating that same line. If these response segments are included in the training dataset, the TTS model will attempt to average the acoustic features of a massive, multi-speaker chorus into a single speaker embedding, resulting in catastrophic model failure.The dataset requires strict speaker diarization to isolate only the target voice. The Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA), which will be used in subsequent alignment steps, includes built-in speaker diarization utilities (mfa diarize_speakers). This process relies on extracting acoustic features (such as MFCCs) and computing x-vectors, which are compact, fixed-length representations of speaker characteristics derived from deep neural networks. By establishing a specific voice print (speaker embedding) for the target historical voice, the pipeline uses agglomerative clustering to automatically identify and discard all audio segments spoken by disciples or audience members. This ensures the acoustic space mapped by the TTS model remains strictly monophonic and singular in its identity.Linguistic Normalization and Forced AlignmentOnce the pristine, isolated, and denoised audio snippets of verse chanting are obtained, they must be perfectly time-aligned with the corresponding text. This process dictates exactly when each phoneme begins and ends within the audio file, a prerequisite for training the duration predictors in advanced TTS architectures. Sanskrit phonology poses highly unique challenges compared to English, necessitating specialized transliteration and alignment models.Orthographic Transliteration and the CLS FrameworkSanskrit text within digital archives is typically presented in either the Devanagari script or the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST), the latter utilizing diacritics to represent specific phonetic variations (e.g., śrī-bhagavān uvāca). For TTS engines, standardizing this orthographic representation into a machine-readable format is paramount.The Python library indic-transliteration is heavily utilized in computational Sanskrit to convert text deterministically across various scripts and encoding formats. This library enables the programmatic conversion of IAST or standard Devanagari into SLP1 (Sanskrit Library Phonetic Basic), ASCII, or customized internal phonetic maps required by the alignment software.Indian languages share a high degree of phonetic consistency due to their shared linguistic origins and structural frameworks. To leverage this, researchers utilize the Common Label Set (CLS) framework, which maps acoustically similar phones across multiple Indian languages into a unified label set. By converting the input Sanskrit verse into an internal phonetic map that aligns with the CLS, the system can leverage broader Indic language acoustic models for initial alignment, preventing the need to train a Sanskrit acoustic model entirely from scratch.The Montreal Forced Aligner and IndicMFAForced alignment generates time-stamped textgrids that segment the audio signal into phonetic units. The Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA), built on the Kaldi automatic speech recognition framework, is the global standard for this task. MFA utilizes a Hidden Markov Model combined with a Gaussian Mixture Model (HMM-GMM) architecture to iteratively align acoustic observations with the phonetic transcript.However, standard Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) models trained on English orthography are highly lossy when applied to Sanskrit. Sanskrit orthography is inherently phonetic; with very few exceptions (such as specific sandhi rules across word boundaries), each grapheme uniquely and consistently represents a specific phonetic sound. Therefore, attempting to train a complex G2P neural network introduces unnecessary error margins.To resolve this, the pipeline should utilize IndicMFA, an optimized framework developed by AI4Bharat specifically for Indian languages. IndicMFA replaces the traditional, error-prone G2P process with a direct Grapheme-to-Grapheme (G2G) mapping. Because the script correlates directly to sound, the pronunciation dictionary simply maps each discrete grapheme to itself, representing a 1:1 translation.The MFA algorithm processes the audio and the G2G dictionary, optimizing the state sequence of the HMM to find the most probable alignment path through the acoustic features. The system evaluates its accuracy using metrics such as Unit Boundary Positioning Accuracy (UBPA), ensuring that the boundaries of phonetic units identified by the system closely match the true acoustic transitions.The output of this process is a Praat .TextGrid file, which segments the target speaker's chanting recordings down to the millisecond boundary of every individual vowel and consonant. This precise, sub-word temporal alignment is strictly required for advanced, non-autoregressive TTS architectures, which rely on external ground-truth durations to train their internal duration predictors.Computational Prosody and the Mathematics of Sanskrit MeterThe core requirement outlined in the user query is that the synthesized voice must chant arbitrary Sanskrit verses in the "proper meter." This is the most complex component of the pipeline. Standard conversational TTS models predict phoneme duration and pitch based on semantic context, punctuation, and the natural conversational cadence learned from the training data. However, classical Sanskrit poetry is not conversational; it is rigidly governed by Chhandas (prosody or meter), an ancient and highly mathematical system that dictates the exact duration (weight) of every syllable in a verse.If a TTS model is not explicitly conditioned on this metrical framework, it will synthesize the verse as prose, entirely failing to capture the musical, rhythmic chanting style of the historical speaker.The Binary System of Laghu and GuruSanskrit meter is quantified by syllable weight, known as mātrā. The system is fundamentally binary, classifying every syllable into one of two categories:Laghu (L): A light or short syllable. A syllable is classified as Laghu if it contains a short vowel (such as a, i, u, ṛ) and is not immediately followed by a consonant cluster. It is assigned a mātrā weight of 1.Guru (G): A heavy or long syllable. A syllable is classified as Guru if it contains a long vowel (such as ā, ī, ū, e, o, ai, au) or if it contains a short vowel that is followed by a conjunct consonant (samyoga), an anusvara (nasalization), or a visarga (aspiration). It is assigned a mātrā weight of 2.The verses of texts like the Bhagavad-gita adhere strictly to specific sequences of Laghu and Guru syllables. For example, the most common meter in the epic is the Anuṣṭubh meter. It consists of four pādas (lines or quarters), each containing exactly eight syllables. The defining characteristic of the Anuṣṭubh meter lies in the specific constraints applied to the 5th, 6th, and 7th syllables of each pāda.Programmatic Meter Identification via ChandojñānamTo programmatically identify and enforce these metrical rules on arbitrary text input, the computational pipeline must utilize a prosodic evaluation engine. Chandojñānam, a Python-based Sanskrit meter identification system developed by researchers at IIT Kanpur, serves exactly this purpose.The Chandojñānam system operates via a highly structured algorithmic methodology:Linguistic Pre-processing and Syllabification: The input verse is transliterated into an internal representation (typically Devanagari) and split into constituent lines based on markers like the danda (।) and double danda (॥). The lines are then split into precise syllables using a customized phonetic parser.Weight Assignment: The system evaluates each syllable against the rules of Sanskrit prosody. It identifies short and long vowels and looks ahead to detect consonant clusters, outputting a binary array of L and G identifiers.Regex Signature Matching: The generated binary string (e.g., LGGLGGLGGLGG for the Bhujaṅgaprayāta meter) is evaluated against a comprehensive metrical database containing over 200 regular expression signatures. The system utilizes direct matching algorithms alongside fuzzy matching (via edit distance) to account for textual anomalies or optical character recognition (OCR) errors.Once the Chandojñānam system processes an input verse, it outputs a deterministic, syllable-by-syllable mapping indicating which units must be short (Laghu) and which must be long (Guru).Injecting Prosodic Conditions into the TTS ArchitectureThis discrete metrical data must be explicitly fed into the TTS model's duration predictor. In advanced non-autoregressive models (such as FastSpeech2), the duration predictor network attempts to output a scalar value representing the number of mel-spectrogram frames for each phoneme.By modifying the TTS architecture to accept a secondary input embedding—a prosodic condition vector corresponding to the Laghu/Guru classification from Chandojñānam—the model is mathematically forced to acknowledge the meter. During the fine-tuning phase on the target speaker's audio, the neural network learns the specific absolute temporal durations that the speaker naturally applies to Laghu versus Guru syllables when chanting in specific rhythms. During inference, when a novel verse is submitted, the Chandojñānam engine dictates the binary metrical array, and the duration predictor scales the predicted length of the phonemes to lock the synthesized speech into perfect, historically accurate rhythmic chanting.Text-to-Speech Architectural Design for Low-Resource Voice CloningSynthesizing natural, high-fidelity speech from a highly limited, niche dataset—specifically, a few hours of extracted verse chanting from a historical figure—demands the implementation of transfer learning and zero-shot capable neural architectures. Training a model from random initialization on such a small dataset would result in catastrophic overfitting and robotic acoustic generation.Neural TTS Architectures for Indic LanguagesRecent computational linguistics literature demonstrates that high-quality Sanskrit TTS can be achieved using remarkably small datasets, provided the architecture supports extensive transfer learning. Debnath et al. successfully synthesized end-to-end Sanskrit TTS using merely 2.5 hours of audio data by fine-tuning a Tacotron 2 model that was originally pre-trained on English.Tacotron 2 utilizes a sequence-to-sequence architecture featuring location-sensitive attention. An encoder processes the character embeddings (often mapped via the Common Label Set to ensure cross-lingual phonetic stability), and a decoder generates a sequence of mel-spectrogram frames autoregressively. This spectrogram is then passed to a neural vocoder, such as WaveGlow or HiFi-GAN, which reconstructs the final time-domain audio waveform. While Tacotron 2 achieves high naturalness scores, its autoregressive nature makes it difficult to explicitly force rigid syllable duration, which is detrimental to enforcing the exact mathematical meter required by the user's project.Consequently, non-autoregressive and flow-based models are vastly superior for this specialized task:FastSpeech 2: This architecture generates audio sequences in parallel, eliminating the sequential bottlenecks of autoregressive models. More importantly, it relies on explicit variance adaptors—specifically pitch, energy, and duration predictors. As detailed in the previous section, by explicitly manipulating the duration predictor using the Laghu/Guru arrays, the machine learning engineer gains surgical control over the chant's rhythm.VITS (Variational Inference with adversarial learning for end-to-end Text-to-Speech): VITS represents the current state-of-the-art paradigm for expressive speech synthesis. It integrates the acoustic modeling phase and the vocoder phase into a single, unified end-to-end framework using normalizing flows and stochastic duration predictors. VITS excels at capturing the micro-prosody, breathiness, and emotional resonance of a speaker, making it highly suitable for replicating the nuanced devotional chanting of a historical figure.Foundational Zero-Shot Models and Hybrid MethodologiesAn alternative approach is to leverage massive, foundational zero-shot models recently released specifically for Indian languages. AI4Bharat has open-sourced the Indic-Parler-TTS model, along with server wrappers developed by the open-source community, such as VEXYL AI. Parler-TTS is a unique architecture trained on massive datasets spanning 20 Indic languages (including Sanskrit). It accepts natural language voice descriptions alongside the text, attempting to generate the requested voice dynamically (e.g., prompting the model for a "deep, resonant male voice").Similarly, Facebook's Massively Multilingual Speech (MMS) models provide robust text-to-speech coverage across hundreds of languages. However, while zero-shot models are excellent for standard conversational synthesis, they cannot reliably replicate the highly specific, identifiable vocal timbre of a historical figure like Srila Prabhupada without extensive fine-tuning, nor can they be easily forced to adhere to strict ancient Sanskrit metrical rules via prompt engineering alone.TTS ArchitectureAdvantages for Chanting SynthesisDisadvantages / Architectural LimitationsTacotron 2 + HiFi-GANProven empirical success on extremely small (2.5h) Sanskrit datasets.Autoregressive generation makes it difficult to explicitly force rigid syllable duration constraints.FastSpeech 2Variance adaptors allow direct injection of Laghu/Guru duration arrays from Chandojñānam.Can suffer from reduced naturalness or robotic tone if pitch/energy adaptors are not perfectly tuned.VITSState-of-the-art naturalness and emotional resonance; single end-to-end architecture.Stochastic duration predictors require complex architectural modification to force strict mathematical meter.Indic-Parler-TTSMassive linguistic pre-training across 20 Indic languages ensures robust phonetic coverage.Prompt-based control is insufficiently deterministic for recreating a specific historical identity and rigid prosody.The Optimal Computational PipelineTo satisfy the stringent requirements of generating proper meter in a specific historical voice, a hybrid methodology is required.First, the system must utilize a pre-trained base model—preferably VITS or FastSpeech 2—that has been trained on a large-scale Indic language dataset. Languages such as Marathi or Telugu are ideal pre-training substrates, as their phonetic inventories and phonotactic rules share significant overlap with Sanskrit, thereby establishing a robust acoustic prior within the network's weights.Second, this base model must undergo a rigorous fine-tuning phase exclusively on the meticulously cleaned, source-separated, and MFA-aligned dataset of the target speaker's isolated verse recitations (scraped from the Vedabase and Vanipedia archives). During this phase, the network learns to map the generic Indic phonemes to the specific vocal tract characteristics, formants, and rhythmic cadence of the target speaker.Finally, during the inference phase, when the user inputs a novel Sanskrit verse, the system parses the text through the Chandojñānam engine to calculate the binary metrical array of Laghu and Guru syllables. This deterministic array is injected directly into the TTS model's duration predictor, modulating the temporal targets before the waveform is synthesized.Mitigation of Technical ChallengesThe deployment of this pipeline requires mitigating two primary linguistic and acoustic challenges: data sparsity regarding complex morphology, and code-switching interference.Morphological Complexity and Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) HandlingClassical Sanskrit features immense morphological complexity, characterized by extensive compound words (samāsa) and complex euphonic combinations of adjacent letters (sandhi). Consequently, an input verse requested by the user may contain highly unique, dense grapheme sequences that the acoustic model has never encountered during training.Because IndicMFA and the recommended TTS models utilize grapheme-to-grapheme processing, complete out-of-vocabulary failure is mitigated at the fundamental character level. However, the model may still struggle to generate smooth acoustic transitions between rare conjunct consonants. To resolve this, the text corpus should be heavily augmented using the raw DokuWiki syntax files from repositories like iskconpress/books to train a highly robust prior language model (LM). This allows the linguistic encoder to better anticipate and process complex consonant clusters prior to acoustic generation.Handling Code-Switching in the Source AudioThe historical audio archives frequently feature the speaker transitioning seamlessly from Sanskrit chanting to English commentary within the same breath or sentence. If these boundary regions are inadvertently included in the training dataset, the acoustic model will suffer from code-switching interference, attempting to learn corrupt phonetic mappings (e.g., applying English phonotactics to Sanskrit text representations).To prevent this, the programmatic scraping and slicing algorithm detailed in Section II must employ advanced energy-based Voice Activity Detection (VAD) coupled with phonetic boundary detection derived from the Montreal Forced Aligner. By analyzing the .TextGrid output, the pipeline can aggressively crop the audio precisely at the millisecond the Sanskrit verse ends and the English commentary begins, ensuring absolute phonetic purity in the training corpus.ConclusionThe objective of constructing a text-to-speech model capable of rendering arbitrary Sanskrit verses in the specific voice of a historical figure—while strictly adhering to classical mathematical prosody—is a highly demanding task, yet it is entirely feasible using modern open-source computational linguistic frameworks.The primary prerequisite of acquiring a ground-truth dataset of annotated audio is resolved by programmatically scraping the Vedabase and Vanipedia archives. By specifically targeting the indexed "His Reciting Bhagavad-gita Verses" metadata and leveraging the pristine, human-verified transcripts from the Audio to Text Fidelity Project, researchers can compile an exceptional corpus of audio-text pairs. The inherent historical degradation of vintage magnetic tape audio must be aggressively counteracted using deep-learning source separation tools like Demucs to remove instrumental bleed, followed by advanced spectral denoisers to eliminate hiss and electrical hum.Crucially, the success of the model hinges on treating Sanskrit not merely as conversational text, but as a rigid musical and mathematical score. By replacing traditional English-centric alignment with IndicMFA's direct grapheme-to-grapheme maps, and explicitly programming metrical constraints (Laghu and Guru) derived from the Chandojñānam prosodic engine into the duration predictors of a non-autoregressive TTS architecture, the resulting synthesis transcends basic voice cloning. This meticulously structured pipeline ensures the preservation of both the unique acoustic signature of the speaker and the timeless rhythmic integrity of classical Sanskrit recitation.
