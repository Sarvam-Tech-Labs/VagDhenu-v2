Computational Architectures and Benchmark Datasets for Sandhi Viccheda and Sandhi Vigraha in Natural Language ProcessingIntroduction to Euphonic Assimilation in Natural Language ProcessingIn the domain of computational linguistics and natural language processing (NLP), the handling of highly inflected, morphologically rich, and agglutinative languages presents profound algorithmic challenges. Among the most complex of these challenges is the resolution of euphonic assimilation at word and morpheme boundaries, a phenomenon universally referred to in Indo-Aryan and Dravidian linguistics as Sandhi. Derived from the Sanskrit root meaning "placing together" or "fusion," sandhi governs the deterministic phonetic transformations that occur when two independent words or morphemes coalesce into a single continuous string.The reverse engineering of this phonetic fusion encompasses two distinct but deeply interrelated computational tasks: Sandhi Viccheda and Sandhi Vigraha. Sandhi Viccheda (the splitting or severing of the euphonic junction) is primarily a tokenization and boundary-detection task, identifying the precise character index where a continuous text string must be fractured. Sandhi Vigraha, conversely, is a deeper morphological analysis task that requires the computational restoration of the original phonemes that were altered, merged, or deleted during the fusion, subsequently mapping these restored forms to valid grammatical stems (padas) in a lexicon.In languages such as Sanskrit, Hindi, Marathi, Malayalam, and Kannada, sandhi processes effectively eliminate explicit word boundaries, resulting in scriptio continua (continuous text). The prevalence of heavily sandhified strings fundamentally disrupts modern, statistically driven subword tokenization algorithms, such as Byte Pair Encoding (BPE). Because BPE and similar tokenizers merge characters based on corpus frequency rather than structural grammar, they fracture complex sandhi strings at mathematically convenient but linguistically invalid statistical seams, outputting junk tokens that degrade the performance of downstream models. Consequently, dedicated sandhi splitting architectures—and the exhaustive benchmark datasets required to train them—are foundational prerequisites for advanced NLP tasks, including morphological tagging, dependency parsing, knowledge graph construction, and machine translation.This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the benchmark datasets, open-source corpora, rule-based algorithms, and state-of-the-art neural frameworks utilized for Sandhi Viccheda and Sandhi Vigraha across Indic languages, focusing on the evolution from deterministic finite-state transducers to modern uncertainty-aware sequence-to-sequence models.The Linguistic Mechanics of Sandhi and SamasaTo fully comprehend the structure of the datasets used to train sandhi splitters, it is necessary to examine the generative rules that govern euphonic fusion. These rules were originally codified by the ancient grammarian Pāṇini in his foundational text, the Aṣṭādhyāyī (circa 500 BCE). Sandhi rules operate as a formal, deterministic phonetic encryption of underlying syntax, which is entirely reversible if the governing context is understood.Typology of Sandhi TransformationsClassical Sanskrit grammar dictates over 280 distinct sandhi protocols. Computationally, these protocols dictate phonetic adjustments that blur lexical boundaries and treat entire sentences as a single character run. They fall into three primary classifications based on the interacting phonemes:Swara (Vowel) Sandhi (Ach Sandhi): This phenomenon occurs when the final character of a preceding word and the initial character of a succeeding word are both vowels. The primary characteristic of Swara Sandhi is the elimination of hiatus. Common algorithmic sub-types include Savarna Deergha (the lengthening of similar vowels, where a/ā + a/ā = ā, e.g., vidyā + ālayaḥ = vidyālayaḥ), Guṇa (where a/ā + i/ī = e, e.g., sūrya + udaya = sūryodaya), Vṛddhi, and Yaṇ sandhis (where i/ī or u/ū transforms into y or v before dissimilar vowels).Vyanjana (Consonant) Sandhi (Hal Sandhi): This occurs when at least one of the adjacent characters at a word boundary is a consonant. It frequently involves complex shifts in voicing, aspiration, or nasalization. For example, the unvoiced velar plosive 'k' transforms into the voiced 'g' before a voiced consonant, yielding vāk + hari = vāgghari.Visarga Sandhi: This transformation occurs when the preceding word terminates in a visarga (an aspirate unvoiced sound, denoted by 'ḥ'). Depending on the succeeding vowel or consonant, the visarga may be dropped entirely, transformed into an 'o', or changed into a sibilant. Classic examples include punaḥ + api = punarapi and namaḥ + te = namaste.Sandhi Viccheda vs. Samasa SplittingA critical distinction in dataset construction and algorithmic processing is the operational difference between Sandhi and Samasa (compounding). While sandhi operates at the phonological level, causing sound shifts exclusively at lexical edges, samasa operates at the morphological-semantic interface. Samasa recursively combines multiple independent lexical items into a single, semantically dense unit, much like the German compounding system, though heavily augmented by internal sandhi.Computationally, samasa is represented as a recursive composition function, where component lexemes are combined via a relational semantic operator (e.g., determinative, possessive, or appositional relations) to form a resultant compound. A unified sandhi-splitter must frequently execute both Samasa resolution and Sandhi Viccheda simultaneously, as compound words are inextricably bound by internal sandhi rules. However, a naive algorithmic split often yields syntactically valid but semantically incorrect morphemes. For instance, the compound tadupāsanīyam can be fractured as tat + upāsanīyam (syntactically and semantically correct) or over-split as tat + up + āsanīyam (syntactically possible but contextually nonsensical). Resolving these combinatorial explosions requires datasets that provide not just isolated word splits, but full contextual environments.Exhaustive Analysis of Sanskrit Benchmark DatasetsThe rapid advancement of data-driven, deep-learning models for sandhi resolution over the past decade relies entirely on the curation of large-scale, high-fidelity parallel corpora. It spans a massive chronological timeline, capturing the linguistic evolution of Sanskrit from late Vedic texts, such as the Upanishads (circa 5th century BCE), up to scientific and classical Sanskrit literature from the 19th century. The defining feature of the DCS is its unparalleled structural depth. Virtually every token within the corpus is provided with interlinear lexical analysis, comprehensive morphological tagging, and manually validated sandhi splits, making it an ideal gold standard for training sequence-to-sequence neural networks. The dataset is published under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives 4.0 International License, ensuring its availability for open academic research.Despite its robust architecture, the DCS presents specific challenges for neural training regimens. During the initial annotation phases of the corpus, forms lacking explicit sandhi (unsandhied forms) were not consistently recorded. This incomplete annotation affects up to 65.8% of words in the DCS, primarily concentrated in classical Sanskrit texts, whereas unsandhied forms are meticulously recorded for most Vedic and some Buddhist texts. This historical data sparsity introduces noise when training strict sequence-to-sequence segmentation models, requiring researchers to augment the data or apply heuristic preprocessing.Sanskrit Voyager and Corpus AggregationsRecent advancements in digital humanities have focused on aggregating the DCS with other disparate digital libraries, including the Göttingen Register of Electronic Texts in Indian Languages (GRETIL), SARIT, the Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon (DSBC), and Muktabodha.This aggregation effort culminated in the Sanskrit Travelogue (or Sanskrit Voyager) project. By harmonizing provenance and formatting across eight digital libraries, researchers compiled a unified raw store of 12,394 distinct works. After deduplication, this massive repository yields over 73 million tokens (words) and more than 9 million unique text segments. Released as TSV shards under a Creative Commons non-commercial license, the unified datasets provide highly structured parallel columns containing the original_text (scriptio continua), cleaned_text, analyzed_text (sandhi-split), stemmed_text, and detailed morphology.Specialized Sanskrit Benchmarks and Auxiliary DatasetsBeyond the DCS and SandhiKosh, researchers frequently utilize combinations of specialized datasets to train high-capacity deep learning models and measure specific edge-case capabilities:The UoH (University of Hyderabad) Corpus: Developed by the Department of Sanskrit Studies under the supervision of Prof. Amba Kulkarni, this vast dataset contains over 100,000 high-quality, verified sandhi examples covering all three major sandhi types (Swara, Vyanjana, Visarga). It serves as the primary training data for numerous neural models due to its sheer scale and grammatical accuracy.SIGHUM and Hackathon Datasets: Introduced by Krishna et al. (2017) and Krishnan et al. (2020), these datasets serve as standard evaluation sets for joint word segmentation and morphological parsing. They specifically test models on highly ambiguous, multi-word fusions that require deep contextual awareness.The Itihasa Corpus: Hosted on GitHub, Itihasa is a parallel translation corpus containing 391,548 aligned sentence pairs, providing a massive scale of processed and split Sanskrit text paired with English translations. This dataset is crucial for training cross-lingual embeddings and bridging sandhi resolution with machine translation.The Multi-Split Benchmark (Frontiers 2026): To address the limitations of single-split evaluation metrics, Ali et al. constructed a specialized multi-split dataset containing over 89,000 compound words. These instances feature extreme complexity, with individual words reaching up to 96 characters in length and containing up to five sequential sandhi split boundaries within a single token.DharmicData JSON Repositories: Maintained on GitHub, repositories like DharmicData and Atmabodha provide meticulously structured JSON arrays containing parsed sacred texts, including the Ramcharitmanas, Bhagavad Gita, Mahabharata, and the Vedas. These repositories are critical for validating sandhi splitters on domain-specific poetic meter.Kaggle Curations: Open repositories, such as the "Sanskrit Sandhi Dataset: Word-Split Pairs," provide highly structured tabular data mapping compound words (e.g., dharmakṣetre) to their grammatically accurate decomposed forms (e.g., dharma + kṣetre), utilizing explicit + delimiters to mark euphonic boundaries. Another dataset, sanskrit-sandhi-boundaries-v2, frames the task as token classification, utilizing multi-dimensional JSON arrays to pinpoint boundary coordinates.Datasets and Methodologies in Modern Indic LanguagesWhile classical Sanskrit represents the pinnacle of complex euphonic fusion, sandhi rules heavily influence modern Indo-Aryan and Dravidian languages. Consequently, parallel dataset generation and algorithmic modeling efforts have expanded to support robust NLP pipelines for these languages.Hindi Sandhi Corpora and Rule-Based SplittingIn Hindi, vocabulary is constructed using a combination of swar (vowels), vyanjan (consonants), and matras (vowel diacritics). While Hindi sandhi rules are less strictly defined and fewer in number than Sanskrit's exhaustive Pāṇinian framework, they remain critical for text analysis.Institutions such as Jawaharlal Nehru University (JNU), under researchers including Dr. Girish Nath Jha, have pioneered the development of extensive Hindi sandhi rule databases and corresponding evaluation corpora. Early rule-based systems mapped Hindi string length, located the position of the matra, and applied reverse heuristic rules in UTF-8 Unicode to decompose compounds. For instance, rules identifying the presence of a half letter (like a half 'ch') combined with specific suffixes would trigger a decomposition sequence. Evaluated on targeted corpora of approximately 887 unique Hindi compound words (e.g., pustak + ālay = pustakālay), these early rule-based algorithmic architectures achieved accuracies ranging between 60% and 80%, depending on the stringency of the rule implementation.Dravidian Languages: Kannada and MalayalamDravidian languages exhibit intense agglutination, often importing Sanskrit vocabulary intact and layering it with regional morphological markers. In Kannada, for example, texts feature two distinct types of sandhi: native Kannada Sandhi and borrowed Sanskrit Sandhi.Specialized datasets comprising thousands of Kannada-Sanskrit sandhi words have been developed specifically to improve Kannada-to-English machine translation frameworks. Unresolved sandhi dramatically increases out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rates, degrading translation quality. To combat this, researchers trained Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) models utilizing an Encoder-Decoder mechanism with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units on custom datasets containing 41,000 pairs of parallel data. The computational demands for processing these morphologically rich translations necessitated 17GB Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) alongside CUDA-enabled GPUs. Splitting these hybrid words prior to translation significantly improved Bi-Lingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU) scores over traditional Statistical Machine Translation (SMT) algorithms like MOSES.Similarly, languages like Marathi and Malayalam utilize proprietary datasets of compound words for rule-based and corpus-based statistical modeling. Marathi sandhi analyzers, evaluated against dictionary lookups and suffix stripping tables, report parsing accuracies of 70–80%. Malayalam, known for its extreme morphological richness, requires highly robust statistical modeling to predict boundaries successfully.Emerging Low-Resource Language DatasetsPlatforms like Hugging Face and Kaggle have catalyzed the release of datasets for under-resourced languages. For instance, Sindhi datasets encompassing legal text, sentiment analysis arrays, and optical character recognition (OCR) image folders for handwritten alphabets provide the foundational raw text necessary for building language models capable of future morphological analysis.Architectural Evolution of Sandhi Splitting ModelsThe methodologies for performing Sandhi Viccheda and Vigraha have evolved significantly, moving through three distinct computational paradigms: Rule-Based/Lexical Finite State Transducers, Statistical and Early Neural approaches, and Modern Transformer/Sequence-to-Sequence models.Rule-Based and Lexical FrameworksEarly computational approaches relied exclusively on the exact computational implementation of Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī as finite-state transducers (FSTs) or regular-expression rewrite rules. Because sandhi generation is a deterministic, finite-state function, early researchers hypothesized that reverse sandhi (splitting) could be achieved merely by inverting these rules alongside exhaustive dictionary lookups.Notable systems in this paradigm include:The Sanskrit Heritage Reader (SHR): Developed by Gérard Huet at INRIA, this highly optimized, lexicon-driven segmenter frames Sanskrit text as a phonetic smoothing problem. It efficiently inverts this smoothing to generate a lattice of all grammatically valid segmentations, utilizing OCaml-based morphological databanks.Samsaadhanii: Developed by Amba Kulkarni at the University of Hyderabad, this comprehensive suite relies heavily on morphological generation and exhaustive rule bases. It parses sentences by analyzing valid padas (inflected words) and generating Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) that map all theoretically possible split paths.JNU Sandhi Tools: A web-based application utilizing Paninian rules translated into standard computational logic (if-then-else conditions) to reverse vocalic and consonant sandhis dynamically.Architectural Limitations: While rule-based systems theoretically guarantee that every generated split is morphologically valid, they suffer from catastrophic combinatorial explosions. A single 10-word string of Sanskrit text can generate hundreds of mathematically valid split combinations. Because these FSTs lack statistical or contextual understanding of semantics, they cannot independently determine which of the 100 valid paths is contextually intended, thereby placing the burden of disambiguation entirely onto the human user. Furthermore, rule-based systems are inherently brittle; if a word contains a typo or utilizes an out-of-vocabulary (OOV) stem, the FST halts entirely and returns zero results.Statistical and Early Neural ModelsTo address the disambiguation problem inherent in rule-based systems, researchers introduced statistical models, such as path-constrained random walks over semantic networks, to assign probabilistic weights to the DAG paths generated by rule-based systems.By 2015, the field began transitioning toward fully data-driven deep learning. Oliver Hellwig introduced a Character-level Recurrent Neural Network (Char-RNN) for Sanskrit segmentation, achieving an impressive string accuracy of 93.24%. This architecture was subsequently upgraded to a hybrid CNN+RNN model by Hellwig and Nehrdich (2018). This hybrid model applied convolutional layers over character embeddings to capture the highly localized, n-gram phonetic shifts occurring at word boundaries, followed by LSTMs for global context, achieving 96.7% character-level accuracy. These models definitively proved that deep learning could bypass external lexical resources entirely, operating solely on parallel sequences of raw and segmented text.Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) ArchitecturesBecause sandhi viccheda fundamentally transforms an input character sequence of length $N$ into an output sequence of length $M$ (where $M > N$ due to the restoration of deleted characters and the insertion of explicit boundary markers), it is naturally formulated as a Sequence-to-Sequence machine translation problem.Aralikatte et al. (2018) revolutionized this approach by introducing the seq2(seq)^2 architecture, specifically tailored for the highly localized nature of Sandhi. Through empirical analysis of the UoH and SandhiKosh datasets, they observed that sandhi transformations rarely affect more than the last two characters of the prefix and the first two characters of the suffix. Leveraging this, their model utilized a two-stage approach:Stage 1 (Boundary Detection): A Bidirectional RNN predicts the precise "sandhi-window"—a contiguous sequence of exactly 5 characters where the euphonic fusion occurred—within the larger compound string.Stage 2 (Splitting): A localized Seq2Seq model, utilizing an attention mechanism and a hidden unit size of 128, takes only this isolated 5-character window and translates it into the two unsandhied morphological components, separated by a '+' delimiter.This heavily constrained neural approach bypassed the need to push infinitely long scriptio continua sentences through a single attention bottleneck. Training on the UoH corpus, this model achieved massive gains in Split Prediction Accuracy (SPA) over both the JNU and UoH baselines, without requiring external dictionaries.State-of-the-Art: Byte-Level Transformers and Uncertainty-Aware ModelsThe advent of massive Transformer architectures fundamentally altered Sandhi processing, allowing for global self-attention over entire paragraphs rather than highly localized sliding windows.ByT5-SanskritStandard Large Language Model (LLM) tokenizers (like WordPiece or SentencePiece) rely on subword vocabulary frequencies, which continuously fracture sandhi braids unpredictably. To circumvent this, Nehrdich et al. (2024) introduced ByT5-Sanskrit, fine-tuning Google's byte-level T5 architecture—a token-free model—on 6.5 billion tokens of Sanskrit text. By operating purely at the byte level, ByT5 intrinsically learns the phonological morphing rules without vocabulary constraints. It achieved 90.11% exact match accuracy on the DCS benchmark and 94.29% on the Hackathon benchmark, representing a leap in zero-shot generalization for combined segmentation and lemmatization tasks.BiLSTM-CRF with Uncertainty-Aware Coupling (Frontiers 2026)Despite achieving high accuracy, standard Transformers and autoregressive models (like recent Gemma-3 adaptations) struggle with "boundary ambiguity" in complex multi-split scenarios. Because sandhi can obscure boundaries entirely, generative models sometimes hallucinate non-existent morphemes or over-split words.To solve this, Ali et al. (2026) proposed a unified framework that jointly models boundary localization (a structured sequence labeling task) and character-level generation (a sequence-to-sequence task). Their architecture utilizes a Transformer encoder whose states $H_{enc}$ are passed into a two-layer Bidirectional LSTM stack to capture explicit left-to-right and right-to-left sequential dynamics.The critical innovation is the application of a Linear-Chain Conditional Random Field (CRF) on top of the BiLSTM emission scores. Instead of simply predicting independent boundary probabilities at each character, the CRF models the transition dependencies between neighboring characters, decoding a globally optimal boundary path.
The CRF computes the probability of a candidate boundary sequence $B$ given the input $X$ as:$$P(B\vert{}X) = \frac{\exp(\sum_{i} \psi(b_{i-1}, b_i, X))}{Z(X)}$$
where $Z(X)$ is the normalization constant and $\psi$ represents the transition log-potentials.Furthermore, the network extracts soft posterior marginals from the CRF (representing the model's computed uncertainty about a specific split). These uncertainty signals are fed through a learned gating mechanism directly into the Transformer decoder, modulating its sequence generation based on how confident the encoder is about the boundary. Therefore, evaluating NLP systems requires specialized metrics that capture both intrinsic accuracy (how well the model fits the data) and extrinsic downstream utility (how useful the split is for the next step in the pipeline).Intrinsic and Deterministic MetricsWhen evaluating a model against a gold-standard dataset like SandhiKosh or DCS, researchers primarily utilize deterministic classification and exact-match metrics:Location Prediction Accuracy (LPA): Measures the percentage of times the model correctly identifies the exact character index where a sandhi split must occur, regardless of whether it subsequently synthesizes the resulting phonemes correctly.Split Prediction Accuracy (SPA): A stricter metric that measures whether the model accurately synthesizes the restored morphemes at the correctly identified boundary.Exact Match (EM) / String Accuracy: A binary metric that returns 1 only if the entire reconstructed multi-word sequence matches the ground truth perfectly, and 0 otherwise. This is highly unforgiving for long sequences but serves as the ultimate test of generative fidelity.Token-Level Precision, Recall, and F1-Score:Precision: The ratio of correctly predicted splits to the total number of predicted splits. High precision penalizes the model for over-splitting.Recall: The ratio of correctly predicted splits to the total number of actual splits in the ground truth. High recall penalizes the model for under-splitting.F1-Score: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a balanced, single-number measure of the model's boundary detection and generation capabilities.Extrinsic and Downstream MetricsBecause Sandhi Viccheda is rarely an end-goal in itself—serving instead as a vital pre-processing step for translation, search, or syntactic parsing—extrinsic metrics are critical for validating real-world model utility.BLEU (Bi-Lingual Evaluation Understudy): Primarily used when the sandhi splitter feeds into a machine translation pipeline. BLEU measures the n-gram overlap between the system's final translation and a human reference. Improved sandhi splitting directly correlates to higher BLEU scores by reducing the OOV burden on the translation encoder.ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation): Evaluates the recall and logical flow (utilizing longest common subsequences) of generated downstream text, ensuring the narrative remains coherent after the splitting process.BERTScore: An embedding-based metric that evaluates the semantic similarity between the model's output and the ground truth. This is particularly useful in Sandhi Vigraha, as it assigns partial credit if a model outputs a syntactically variant but semantically synonymous stem, whereas Exact Match would score it as a total failure.The Role of Baselines in Benchmark EvaluationThe immense utility of structured datasets like SandhiKosh lies in their ability to establish strict baseline model performance. In NLP governance, model auditing, and architecture validation, baselines—such as simple dictionary-based splitting, JNU rule-based FSTs, or un-tuned sequence-to-sequence models—serve to anchor expectations and quantify true algorithmic progress. By continuously comparing novel architectures against these established baselines, researchers can objectively measure the necessary trade-offs between computational latency, memory footprint, and exact-match accuracy, ensuring that models do not become overly complex without yielding tangible gains.Downstream Applications and Knowledge Graph ExtractionThe accurate computational resolution of Sandhi is the gateway to unlocking the vast, underexplored repositories of historical, philosophical, and scientific knowledge encoded in classical Indic texts.Digital Humanities and Corpus Search PlatformsAdvanced splitting models are currently being integrated directly into interactive reading and analysis platforms. The Sanskrit Voyager and Dharmamitra platforms leverage models like ByT5-Sanskrit to provide real-time, interactive text reading interfaces. Users can query a massive corpus for a specific lemma (root word), and the platform will dynamically de-sandhi and normalize all scriptio continua in the database to find exact semantic matches. This bridges the significant gap between raw manuscript digitization and highly searchable, linguistically tagged knowledge bases. Modern APIs allow developers to integrate these capabilities directly into mobile applications, such as the Samsaadhanii app suite, which provides on-the-fly morphological analysis and sandhi joining/splitting for students and researchers.Ontological Mapping and Knowledge GraphsSandhi splitting is an absolute prerequisite for automated Knowledge Graph (KG) construction from classical literature. In specialized scientific domains, such as Ayurvedic medical texts (e.g., the Bhāvaprakāśanighaṇṭu), entities and relations are densely fused through complex grammatical structures. A sentence describing the properties of a medicinal plant must first be split accurately before downstream Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Relation Extraction (RE) models can map the syntax into an ontological lattice.For example, when constructing a knowledge graph containing hundreds of entities (e.g., pitta, snigdha) and relationships (e.g., "is a disease of", "is cured by"), an error in the initial splitting phase propagates exponentially. If the sandhi splitter fails—such as erroneously splitting the compound rāmalakṣmaṇau into two disjointed nominatives rather than recognizing the single dual compound—the resulting knowledge graph will contain severe relational hallucinations, destroying the structural integrity of the extracted data. The emergence of highly accurate, uncertainty-aware neural splitters mitigates this risk, enabling the reliable extraction of ancient scientific data into modern, machine-readable formats.ConclusionThe computational processing of Sandhi Viccheda and Sandhi Vigraha represents a critical intersection between ancient linguistic formalism and modern deep learning architectures. Early reliance on the deterministic beauty of Pāṇinian rules produced highly precise but computationally rigid finite-state transducers that faltered in the face of ambiguity, out-of-vocabulary terms, and non-standard inputs.The introduction of standardized, exhaustively annotated benchmark datasets—most notably the SandhiKosh corpus and Oliver Hellwig's massive Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS)—precipitated a fundamental paradigm shift toward data-driven, sequence-to-sequence modeling. By providing hundreds of thousands of parallel, manually validated mapping sequences, these datasets allowed researchers to transition from inflexible heuristic rules to fluid neural networks capable of contextual disambiguation.Today, state-of-the-art architectures, such as the byte-level ByT5-Sanskrit and joint BiLSTM-CRF boundary models, successfully navigate the dense, scriptio continua nature of Sanskrit, Hindi, Kannada, and other morphologically rich languages. By treating euphonic fusion not merely as a rule-based puzzle, but as a probabilistic sequence generation task strictly bounded by grammatical uncertainty, these models have achieved unprecedented exact-match accuracies consistently exceeding 90%. As these datasets continue to expand to include highly complex multi-split phenomena, and as evaluation metrics become increasingly aligned with human semantic understanding, the computational barrier to advanced natural language processing, machine translation, and knowledge extraction for classical languages continues to diminish rapidly.
